# I built Woon and waited

*Building the thing and giving it a chance turned out to be two different jobs. I only knew how to do one of them.*

A few years ago, I built Woon, an agent that lived inside iMessage. I have mentioned it before, in the manifesto and in the first note of this series, usually in a sentence or two on the way to something else.

This time I want to stay with it. I notice that I keep avoiding it, and I keep finding other people having the conversation I was never part of.

## What I thought the job was

I believed the hard part was making it exist.

If the product worked and the idea mattered, I assumed the right people would eventually find it. "Build it and they will come" was not a slogan I repeated. It was simply the model I was operating with.

They did not come.

Maybe Woon was too early. Maybe it was not good enough. I still do not have a clean answer, and I am not going to manufacture one here. I can see one failure much more clearly now: I never put the idea into circulation.

I did not know where people were already discussing the problem. I did not know how to find the few people who would immediately understand what an agent inside a messaging app could become. I did not know how to express the idea in a form that could move through those conversations, collect criticism, and come back sharper than it left.

I had treated building the thing and giving the thing a chance as one job. They are two.

## The conversation I keep finding

Founders are having a version of this conversation in many places at once. Two threads on Indie Hackers hold both sides of it. [1] [2]

The first is titled "I can build anything. Distribution is the wall I keep hitting." The argument inside it is that broad posting does little, and that useful replies and repeated presence in the right community do more. It also separates engagement from demand. People talking with you is not the same as people wanting what you made.

The second pushes back. Building in public works as distribution for founders who sell to other founders, because founders are the ones reading. When your buyers do not spend time in founder networks, a careful public practice can still be happening in the wrong room.

Read together, they agree on less than it first appears. Both accept that distribution is hard. They split on where the effort belongs. One says to show up usefully and often. The other says to first make sure you are standing where the people you need actually are.

That disagreement is more useful to me than either answer alone.

## Where I sit in it

Woon failed both tests, and I did not know either test existed.

I was not showing up anywhere. Had I tried, I could not have told you which room held the people who would understand an iMessage agent early. I was missing presence, and I was missing the map that tells presence where to go.

Having something worth saying and knowing how to make it travel are separate skills. The network tends to reward the second one first. Some people are fluent in it. They reply quickly, know which conversation matters, and know how to reach someone without losing the thread. I am not naturally that kind of person.

For a long time I treated that as a personal limitation. Now I see it as a product problem.

## What an agent could have done

Imagine Woon had arrived with an agent for its own participation.

It could have found the small pockets of people already building conversational interfaces. It could have shown me which of those rooms held people who might actually use an agent in their messages, and which only held people who liked talking about it. It could have helped me translate the product into the language of each conversation and pointed to where my own experience added something.

That is the lens and the megaphone from the first note, applied to a real failure. The lens finds the room. The megaphone helps an established idea show up usefully once I am in it.

It would not have made Woon better. It would not have created demand that did not exist, and it should never count replies as proof that someone wants the product. It should not have pretended to be me or sprayed automated replies across the internet. The decision to enter a conversation, and every word that went out under my name, would still have been mine.

It could have made the network legible enough for the idea to get a fair test.

## What I still do not know

I do not know whether Woon would have survived meeting its network. It may have failed faster, and that would have been a better outcome than fading out unseen.

I also do not yet know whether agents are good at finding the right room. The software I am building to try is early. The part that watches outside conversations is still working from a small set of sources gathered by hand, including the two threads above. Two threads are a starting point, not a picture of what founders believe.

I would rather learn those answers in public than wait for them again.

## The invitation

What did you build that never found its network?

When you look back, was the problem that you were not showing up, or that you were showing up in the wrong room? Reply and tell me.
